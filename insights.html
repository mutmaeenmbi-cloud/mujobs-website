Notes from inside
the search.

Practical thinking on passive talent, sourcing, specialist hiring and the DACH recruitment market.

LATEST / 2026
PASSIVE TALENT · 6 MIN

The best candidates are often the hardest to reach.

Why strong people disappear from traditional applicant funnels — and what changes when the search starts outside the inbox.

The strongest candidate for a role is not always the person actively applying. Often, they are already employed, performing well and not scrolling through job boards.

A job ad asks people to come to you. Direct search means going to the market yourself: mapping relevant companies, alternative titles and adjacent profiles, then approaching the right people with a reason to have a conversation.

Good recruitment is not about convincing everyone to leave. It is about understanding what could genuinely matter to the right person. When a role is difficult to fill, the answer is rarely simply more applications. Sometimes you need to search beyond the inbox.

SOURCING · 5 MIN

Job ads create applicants. Search creates access.

When proactive market mapping becomes more useful than waiting for another application.

A job advertisement shows you who is looking. It does not show you everyone who could be right for the role.

Proactive sourcing turns the brief into a market map: relevant companies, titles, locations, career paths and adjacent talent pools. The aim is not more messages. It is fewer, more relevant conversations with people the application funnel may never reach.

Applications still matter. But when the right people are not entering the funnel, waiting longer does not necessarily improve it. Search creates access to the market outside the inbox.

REAL ESTATE · 7 MIN

Hiring inside a small specialist talent market.

How geography, project type, ownership and adjacent experience shape a real-estate search.

Real-estate recruitment becomes difficult quickly because a familiar title can describe very different work. Two project developers can have completely different exposure depending on their employer, projects and stage of development.

A useful search goes below the title: acquisition, feasibility, approvals, development concepts, commercial evaluation, project scale and whether the experience comes from a developer, investor, planner or execution environment.

Geography matters too. Local relationships with municipalities, owners and stakeholders can be part of a candidate’s value. Strong shortlists combine role evidence, project context, geography and adjacent experience rather than treating matching titles as interchangeable.

PIPELINES · 5 MIN

The hire is not the only asset a search creates.

A qualified passive pipeline can make the next hiring problem significantly easier.

A successful search ends with a hire, but the work completed before that point has value beyond one placement. The market has already been mapped and relevant people have already been identified.

Some candidates are interested but not ready. Others are strong but slightly wrong for the current brief. Keeping that knowledge means the next vacancy does not have to begin from zero.

A useful pipeline captures relevance, experience, location and previous conversations. Over time, sourcing becomes reusable market knowledge rather than a one-off list of names.

QUALIFICATION · 6 MIN

Titles match. Evidence decides.

Why responsibilities, project context and outcomes matter more than a familiar job title.

Searching by title is useful for finding people. It is much less useful for deciding whether they are actually right for the role.

The same title can represent different levels of ownership between companies. Qualification therefore needs evidence: what did the person own, which projects did they work on, what decisions were they responsible for and how close is that experience to the new employer’s problem?

The title helps build the first search. The evidence determines who belongs on the shortlist. That distinction removes many false positives before outreach begins.

DACH · 6 MIN

One region, different talent markets.

Why a search in Switzerland cannot simply be copied and pasted into Germany.

Germany and Switzerland share a language across large parts of the market and often use similar job titles. That can make a cross-border search look easier than it is.

Company structures, terminology, salary expectations, geography, commuting patterns and the importance of local networks can change the candidate pool. The target companies, title variations and search radius therefore need to change with the market.

DACH is useful as a commercial label. For sourcing, it is better treated as connected but distinct talent markets. The search becomes stronger when the map reflects those differences.
